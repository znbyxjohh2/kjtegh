What happens to iron during pregnancy

Pregnancy triggers a demand for iron unlike anything your body normally faces. Your blood volume increases by approximately 1.5 liters, and every new red blood cell needs hemoglobin, a protein that relies entirely on iron to carry oxygen.

The fetus actively pulls iron from your stores to build its own blood supply. It also stockpiles reserves for the first months of life, when breast milk alone won't deliver enough. Your body honors fetal needs first, even if that depletes you to dangerously low levels.

Why demand spikes in the third trimester

Iron absorption from food does increase during pregnancy. But it can't keep pace with what your body actually needs. The requirement climbs from roughly 0.8 mg of absorbed iron daily in the first trimester to more than 6 mg in the third, a nearly eightfold increase, per Bothwell's foundational analysis in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition.

Most women start pregnancy with marginal iron stores. Heavy periods before pregnancy, short intervals between pregnancies, and vegetarian or vegan diets all chip away at reserves before conception even happens.

How iron deficiency affects you and your baby

Iron deficiency during pregnancy isn't a minor inconvenience. When hemoglobin drops, your blood carries less oxygen, and your body compensates by working harder. That explains the rapid heartbeat, the pounding when you climb stairs, the fatigue that doesn't lift after sleep.

Iron deficiency anemia affects roughly 30–40% of pregnant women globally, per WHO data. The consequences extend well beyond feeling tired.

Effects on your health

Your brain is particularly sensitive to reduced oxygen. Difficulty concentrating, poor memory, and mental fog are direct effects of low iron, not just pregnancy brain. The exhaustion runs deeper too: a bone-level tiredness that rest doesn't fix and worsens as the day goes on.

Iron-deficient women also face higher rates of postpartum hemorrhage, a systematic review and meta-analysis in the Journal of Obstetrics and Gynaecology Research found prepartum anemia increased PPH odds by roughly 45%, and slower recovery after delivery. Iron deficiency impairs immune function, raising infection risk during the already-vulnerable postpartum period.

Impact on fetal brain development

The developing brain has the highest iron demand of any fetal organ, especially during the third trimester when neural connections form rapidly. A 2023 narrative review in International Journal of Gynecology & Obstetrics found that insufficient iron during this window is associated with cognitive and neurobehavioral delays, including impaired memory, processing speed, and executive function, that can persist into childhood and beyond.

Maternal iron deficiency is also associated with increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight, a systematic review and meta-analysis found a relative risk of 1.56 (95% CI 1.25–1.95) for preterm birth among anemic mothers. Babies born to iron-deficient mothers often arrive with depleted stores, setting them up for iron deficiency anemia in infancy, when brain development is still moving fast.

Postpartum complications

Anemia makes the newborn period harder in every direction. The physical recovery is slower. The immune system is weaker. And the bone-deep exhaustion of iron deficiency compounds the sleep deprivation of caring for a newborn into something unsustainable.

Continuing iron supplements after delivery, typically for at least three months, helps your body fully recover before the next pregnancy.

What the evidence says about supplementing

The recommendation to supplement is not precautionary, it's evidence-based. A Cochrane review of 44 trials found that daily iron supplementation during pregnancy reduces the risk of maternal anemia at term by roughly 70% and significantly lowers rates of low birth weight.

The WHO recommends 30–60 mg of elemental iron daily for all pregnant women, starting as early as possible in gestation. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists sets the standard at 27 mg daily, the amount in most prenatal vitamins, and prescribes 60–120 mg daily when deficiency is confirmed by testing.

Daily vs. intermittent dosing

Whether daily or intermittent dosing better prevents anemia is more nuanced than it appears. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis in eClinicalMedicine found no significant difference in end-of-treatment hemoglobin between the two regimens, though daily dosing did achieve higher ferritin concentrations. Intermittent dosing, two to three times per week, may improve per-dose iron absorption by allowing hepcidin, a liver-derived hormone that suppresses intestinal iron uptake, to return to baseline between doses; Moretti et al. demonstrated this mechanism in iron-depleted women in blood (2015). Intermittent dosing also produces significantly fewer gastrointestinal side effects, which matters for compliance.

Consistent supplementation throughout pregnancy matters more than the exact schedule. Supplementing only when you feel bad misses the point. Your ferritin can be depleted long before symptoms appear.

How much iron you need and when to take it

Recommended doses

Standard prenatal vitamins contain 27 mg of elemental iron, enough for pregnant women without diagnosed deficiency. When iron deficiency is confirmed, providers prescribe therapeutic doses of 60–120 mg of elemental iron daily until ferritin and hemoglobin normalize.

The form you take matters for both effectiveness and tolerability:

Ferrous sulfate: Most widely prescribed; a 325 mg tablet delivers ~65 mg elemental iron. Effective, but higher rates of GI side effects.

Ferrous fumarate: 33% elemental iron by weight; slightly better tolerated than ferrous sulfate.

Ferrous gluconate: 12% elemental iron; gentlest on the stomach, but lower concentration per dose.

Iron bisglycinate chelate: Less elemental iron per dose, but a systematic review of RCTs found higher bioavailability per mg and consistently fewer gastrointestinal side effects compared to iron salts. A reasonable alternative if standard iron causes persistent side effects.

Consult your healthcare provider before taking any iron supplement beyond your prenatal vitamin. They can confirm deficiency through testing and recommend the appropriate form and dose for your situation.

Timing and absorption

Iron absorbs best on an empty stomach, but that often worsens nausea in pregnancy. Taking it with a small amount of food reduces side effects without dramatically cutting absorption.

Take iron with vitamin C or a glass of orange juice to enhance uptake. Separate it by at least two hours from calcium-rich foods, dairy, antacids, and coffee or tea, all of which interfere with iron absorption.

Liquid and alternative forms

Women who struggle with pills or have severe nausea may tolerate liquid iron better than tablets. Chewable tablets are another option, though they can temporarily stain teeth. Intravenous iron is reserved for severe deficiency, malabsorption conditions, or when oral iron fails to raise levels despite consistent supplementation.

Why your iron needs may differ

Your starting iron status at conception shapes everything. A woman who enters pregnancy with ferritin below 30 µg/L already has depleted stores, the 27 mg in her prenatal vitamin won't be enough to catch up.

Several factors raise your risk of iron deficiency during pregnancy:

Short pregnancy intervals: Not enough time between pregnancies to rebuild iron stores

Multiple pregnancy: Twins or triplets dramatically increase iron demand throughout gestation

Vegetarian or vegan diet: Non-heme iron from plants absorbs less efficiently than heme iron from animal sources

History of heavy periods: Often signals pre-existing low iron stores at the start of pregnancy

Celiac disease or IBD: Gastrointestinal conditions that impair iron absorption significantly

Genetic variations: Some women absorb iron less efficiently due to inherited differences in iron metabolism

Who should use extra caution

Women with hemochromatosis or other iron overload disorders must not supplement without direct medical supervision.

Those taking thyroid medication should separate it from iron by at least four hours, iron blocks thyroid hormone absorption.

Certain antibiotics interact with iron; discuss timing with your doctor or pharmacist.

Signs you may be iron-deficient

Fatigue is the most common symptom of iron deficiency during pregnancy, and the most easily dismissed. Iron deficiency fatigue feels different from normal pregnancy tiredness: deeper, unresponsive to rest, and worse as the day progresses.

Watch for these other signs:

Shortness of breath with minimal exertion

Heart racing or pounding, especially on stairs

Lightheadedness or dizziness when standing quickly

Unusual paleness in nail beds, inner eyelids, or palms

Smooth, pale tongue, loss of the normal papillae texture

Pica, cravings for ice, dirt, or starch, which strongly suggests iron deficiency

Brittle nails or hair loss beyond normal pregnancy shedding

Restless legs syndrome

Frequent headaches and difficulty concentrating

Feeling cold even in warm environments

These symptoms develop gradually. That's exactly what makes them easy to blame on pregnancy itself. Blood testing is the only way to confirm what's actually happening.

How to track your iron status

Symptoms reveal problems only after they're already serious. Testing can help identify low iron levels before symptoms develop, and before they affect your baby.

Here's what to track:

Hemoglobin: The primary anemia marker. Below 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters (below 10.5 g/dL in the second) defines anemia in pregnancy.

Hematocrit: Measures the proportion of red blood cells in your blood, complementing hemoglobin readings.

Ferritin: Your iron storage marker. Below 30 µg/L indicates deficiency, even when hemoglobin is still normal. Below 15 µg/L is severe depletion.

Serum iron: Reflects how much iron is circulating at the time of testing; best interpreted alongside ferritin.

Total iron binding capacity (TIBC): Rises when iron is low, helping distinguish iron deficiency from other causes of anemia.

Iron saturation: Falls below 16% in iron deficiency; confirms the picture alongside ferritin and TIBC.

Test early in pregnancy to establish your baseline. Test again at 28 weeks, when iron demand peaks. Tracking these markers over time tells you whether your supplementation is working, or whether you need to adjust.

Standard prenatal doses of 27–60 mg elemental iron are safe for the developing baby. Very high doses above 120 mg daily may increase oxidative stress, so stay within what your provider prescribes. Iron toxicity from routine supplementation is rare, but keep supplements locked away from children, as accidental overdose in young kids is a medical emergency.

How long does it take for iron supplements to work during pregnancy?

Hemoglobin typically begins rising within two to three weeks of starting supplementation, with maximum response at four to eight weeks. Replenishing depleted ferritin stores takes longer, often two to three months of consistent supplementation. Don't stop early just because you feel better. Your stores need time to fully rebuild, and running out again puts both you and your baby at risk.

Should I take extra iron if my prenatal vitamin already contains it?

If your hemoglobin and ferritin are normal, the 27 mg in your prenatal is likely sufficient. If blood tests show iron deficiency or anemia, your provider will prescribe a separate iron supplement, typically 60–120 mg of elemental iron daily, in addition to your prenatal, not instead of it. Testing is the only way to know which situation you're in.

What's the difference between ferritin and hemoglobin testing?

Hemoglobin tells you whether you have anemia right now. Ferritin tells you whether a deficiency is developing before anemia appears. You can have low ferritin with normal hemoglobin, your stores are depleted, but your body is still compensating by pulling from reserves. By the time hemoglobin drops, you've usually been iron-deficient for weeks. Testing both markers gives you the full picture.

Can I take iron supplements in the first trimester if I have morning sickness?

Yes, but iron can aggravate nausea. If standard tablets worsen morning sickness, try taking them with food, switching to iron bisglycinate (a gentler form), or taking your dose before bed. Some women tolerate liquid iron better than pills. If nausea is severe, ask your provider about every-other-day dosing, which may improve tolerance while still delivering meaningful benefit.

Do I need to continue iron supplements after delivery?

Most women should continue for at least three months postpartum to replenish stores depleted during pregnancy and offset blood lost during delivery. Significant bleeding or breastfeeding may extend that timeline. Postpartum blood testing confirms when your levels have fully recovered and it's safe to stop.

Can iron supplements cause constipation during pregnancy?

Yes, constipation is the most common side effect of iron supplementation, particularly with ferrous sulfate. Switching to iron bisglycinate or a polysaccharide-iron complex usually reduces this significantly. Increasing water and fiber intake helps too. If constipation is severe, your provider may suggest a different iron form or an every-other-day schedule until your body adjusts.

What iron supplement form is easiest on the stomach during pregnancy?

Iron bisglycinate chelate and ferrous gluconate are the gentlest forms on the stomach during pregnancy; ferrous sulfate is the most widely prescribed but causes the highest rate of GI side effects. Ferrous gluconate contains roughly 12% elemental iron by weight — the lowest concentration but easiest to tolerate — while iron bisglycinate studies show higher bioavailability per milligram with fewer GI complaints. Intermittent dosing two to three times per week can also reduce side effects by allowing hepcidin to drop between doses. The key is choosing the gentlest form that still meets your prescribed elemental-iron target; discuss the best option with your provider.
