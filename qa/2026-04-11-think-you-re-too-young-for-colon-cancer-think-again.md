# Think You’re Too Young for Colon Cancer? Think Again.

Dave Runkel went to his primary care physician with a problem: the 36-year-old engineer was passing blood. He was a Navy vet living in South Carolina with a career that required frequent overseas travel. His wife also worked full-time, and together they were raising three kids between the ages of 6 and 14. Seeing blood in his stool was disturbing to Runkel, because he’d intermittently had extremely loose stools and searing abdominal pain for nearly a decade—but none of his doctors had ever taken it seriously. Runkel went to the doctor determined to get a colonoscopy. The doctor hesitated—Runkel was younger than the screening age of 45, and colorectal cancer is still relatively rare at his age. Runkel became frustrated and decided to lie to the doctor and tell him he had a family history of cancer. The doctor ordered the test. When he finally got the colonoscopy several months later, the doctors found colorectal cancer.

Living with a cancer diagnosis in his 30s was complicated. Runkel would go to work in the morning, work until four PM, and then head to the clinic to receive intra-rectal radiation. Then he started eight rounds of chemotherapy that consisted of a FOLFOX infusion—a portable balloon pump containing multiple chemotherapy drugs—in the middle of the week. On Friday, after the pump was removed, he would sleep for fourteen hours, feel horrible until Sunday evening, and return to work on Monday. (Since memory loss was a side effect of his chemotherapy, Runkel’s coworkers supply details of events that happened during “the bad times.”)

Then he had surgery to remove the tumor and an ileostomy procedure, where the intestine is routed through the abdomen and empties into a bag. Two months later, he underwent another surgery to reverse the ileostomy. Among all the uncomfortable drugs and medical procedures, the part that bothered Runkel the most was the extra stress cancer put on his wife and kids. And he wondered about how his life would have been different if an earlier doctor had just ordered him a colonoscopy.

“I try not to think of what would have happened if they had found this at the beginning,” says Runkel. “I find that if I just dwell on that part of the story, then it's just that you're angry.”

Tracing Clues to Who's At Risk

Scientists are racing to understand and respond to the rise in early-onset colorectal cancer, which has nearly doubled since 1995, to help find patients like Runkel sooner. According to new data from the University of Missouri, since 1990, patients aged 20 to 24—that’s when Runkel first had symptoms—saw a 185 percent increase in cases, while patients in his age group of 35 to 39 saw a 58.1 percent increase. Even more disturbing is that people with colon cancer that develops this early often seem otherwise super healthy. Now, early onset colorectal cancer centers have opened across the United States, aimed at treating the unique needs of young patients and performing research to better understand the disease.

But that’s treatment. A big question that remains and gnaws at researchers is this: How can we find these younger patients before their disease spreads? Scientists’ goal is to identify what is unique about patients with earlier onset, so they can increase screening efforts in the people who are likely to develop this cancer.

Recently, a fascinating new discovery has gotten researchers closer to identifying who to screen, and it’s redefining the research landscape. In the past few years, some studies had already identified moderate associations between obesity, diabetes, sugar-sweetened beverages, and a sedentary lifestyle and the risk of developing colorectal cancers, but no single finding seemed to explain the increase. Researchers at the University of California San Diego (UCSD) set out to look for differences in who got this cancer and who didn’t by sequencing the DNA from colorectal cancer tumors.

They discovered what is called a “mutational signature” in earlier onset cases like Runkel’s. Different carcinogens generate different patterns of DNA mutations; these allow researchers to group patients with similar exposures like cigarette smoking. The UCSD researchers, part of a large international research group called team Mutographs, are deciphering the unique mutational processes of different cancer types. What they found in this case is that the young patients shared a mutational signature already known to be caused by a mutation-inducing toxin, colibactin. Colibactin is produced by a specific strain of E. coli. bacteria in the colon. These E. coli harbor a polyketide synthase gene which enables the production of this colibactin toxin (the E. coli are called pks+ E. coli). Men and women with colorectal cancer under 40 were 3.3 times more likely to have this mutational signature than patients over 70.

This exciting new finding was so impactful that the journal Nature designated it for accelerated article preview in April 2025. Maybe it could turn out to unlock which people are at risk for cancer. “We could try to find ways to detect this mutation, which is kind of the consequence of the infection,” says Marcos Díaz-Gay, PhD, the first author on the study and now a Junior Group Leader at the Spanish National Cancer Research Center. But even better would be actually trying to detect the infection when it’s happening, he says, which the authors think could be during childhood. While colibactin testing exists, ordering it in the clinic is a few steps away. And knowing who to test for this strain of E.coli in the first place, long before they come to a clinic with cancer, could be a huge leap forward. “From a research perspective, we would like to understand if there are certain environments, diets, lifestyle, behaviors that might be more related with this colibactin,” says Dr. Díaz-Gay.

What Else Could it Be?

As exciting as these findings are, they’re only a piece of the puzzle, since colibactin exposure was only associated with less than 20 percent of colorectal cancer cases in the study. Kimmie Ng, MD, MPH, the founder of the Young-Onset Colorectal Cancer Center at Dana-Farber Cancer Institute, is focused on another aspect of the rise. She’s studying the risks of different environmental exposures, similar to how lung cancer screening uses smoking as a risk factor to identify people who should be screened.

Among the possible contributors to young-onset colon cancer are the ideas that it’s driven by the environment outside of you. “We think that this whole process is very complex, and related to some environmental driver,” said Dr. Ng. “It does not seem to be any shift in genetics or change in family history or inflammatory bowel disease rates rising.” She cites the birth cohort effect, a phenomenon where a group of people born in the same era, with the same exposures in their early life, have increased rates of disease, as evidence for an environmental change behind the increase.

There’s also a theory that it might have to do with changes in the environment inside of you. Cancer researchers call this the “seed vs. soil” hypothesis, where the cancer cell is the seed and the surrounding cells, including immune cells and other resident cells, are the soil. Ken Lau, PhD, a Professor of Cell and Developmental Biology and Surgery at Vanderbilt University, hypothesizes that the seeds—cells with specific mutations with a small chance of becoming cancer—lie dormant until the soil is fertile.

“The idea is that all of us probably have abnormal cells, and they lie dormant for long periods of time before they become activated to first create a polyp, then some of these polyps become cancer,” said Dr. Lau. The question researchers are chasing is: “What are the factors that shorten this dormant time and allow these abnormal cells to grow, to proliferate?” he adds.

To help answer the question of what makes the soil fertile, and why that’s happening more frequently in young people, researchers are finding ways to better understand normal colon cells in young people. William Grady, MD, the Medical Director of the Gastrointestinal Cancer Prevention Program at Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, is interested in profiling colon cells to understand if young patients’ colons are aging at a more rapid pace than in the past. They might be.

Dr. Grady is looking at epigenetic modifications to figure this out. Epigenetic modification is a process of reversible chemical modifications to the DNA which alter gene expression. What that means: They act like an on-off switch. For example, when a methyl group is added to DNA, that gene will be turned off. These changes can be correlated with biological age through grip strength, lung function, and other tests. So far, Dr. Grady’s team found that in people with early onset colorectal cancer, “their epigenetic age is actually even higher than people who have late onset colorectal cancer. It suggests that something's causing an accelerated biological aging in those younger individuals.”

It’s possible, he thinks, that biological aging is associated with metabolic syndrome—that’s when people have three or more of these symptoms at the same time: high blood pressure, high blood sugar, high triglycerides, low HDL cholesterol, and abdominal obesity. Metabolic syndrome incidence is significantly increasing in people aged 20 to 39. “We're wondering if something's happening that's also just accelerating the biological age in general, which then would increase the cancer risk,” says Dr. Grady.

What to Do Now

Despite the increase in risk, young-onset colorectal cancer is still relatively rare. People who are experiencing symptoms like blood in their stool, a change in bowel habits, abdominal pain or unexplained weight loss should see a doctor – and you may need to seek a second opinion if you feel dismissed. “We know colorectal cancer, when caught early, has a good survival rate, and that’s true even for younger patients,” says Joel Gabre, MD, one of the directors of Columbia University’s Early Onset Colorectal Cancer Program.

But for young people with no symptoms, Dr. Grady recommends putting your energy into lowering your cancer risk until we know more about whom to test for what. “Focus on the things you can do to live a healthy lifestyle: exercise regularly; maintain a normal body weight; eat a healthy diet, which is mainly avoiding red meat and processed foods and eating more whole foods; and avoid tobacco exposure.”

“The research that’s needed most is the risk stratification research—who do we screen,” says Dr. Gabre. By getting to the root of who’s at risk, we’ll know whom to screen, and ideally, find people like Runkel faster. In the meantime, don’t ignore new symptoms. It could save your life.

New Tech, New Hope

These technologies are moving science closer to knowing why colon cancer is rising in young people.

Exposomics

To accelerate discovery, scientists are determined to gather as much data as possible about people with young-onset colorectal cancer. One study, the Beyond CRC Project from Dana-Farber, will collect samples of blood, stool, and tumor from all new young-onset patients. These will be paired with exposome data: validated diet, lifestyle, and environmental exposure questionnaires. They will also collect data from healthy controls and older colorectal cancer patients to make clear comparisons about what is unique in younger patients. To understand the increase globally, they’re enrolling patients from Spain, Brazil, and around the world.

Spatial transcriptomics

Scientists are using this technology to help build a comprehensive map of what’s happening in the colon. The milieu around tumor cells—called the tumor microenvironment—is made up of many other types of normal, native cells, as well as blood vessels and structural scaffolding. Cancer cells, determined to grow bigger, send signals to these other cells. These shut down opposing immune cells, build new blood vessels, and gain fuel and space so they can keep growing. Spatial transcriptomics shows us where all those players are at a specific time, almost like a freeze-frame instant replay. Tracking the complex interactions of all these components could reveal dynamics that could be game changers. For instance, some tumors have very active immune cells while others don’t. Understanding why some patients have “hotter” tumors than others could be a key to improving treatment.

AI

Since the locations of cancer cells and the tumor microenvironment are so important, scientists want to see these interactions in three dimensions. Traditional spatial transcriptomics looks at only one slice of tumor in two dimensions, but Dr. Lau’s research team at Vanderbilt University aims to look at multiple levels of tissue. To splice them together into a three-dimensional reconstruction, the team will use AI. This 3D data will be used in a large study called the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN). As in other studies, this team is focused on comparing young-onset patients to healthy controls. Another clue in why tumors develop in young patients could be found in pre-cancerous tumors, which they plan to study using the 3D reconstructions. The HTAN hopes to understand how the organization of cells in 3D space could be key to why some patients develop cancer and others don’t.

This way of studying the microbiome allows researchers to get a read on how the microbiome is different between younger and older patients. In Dr. Gabre’s Columbia lab, they are using metagenomics to study not just one type of microbe, but the whole population at once. Think of this like fishing: instead of just fishing for one type of fish with a specific lure, a net will catch all the fish in an area at once. Similarly, metagenomics will find and identify all the diverse bacteria species at once using sequencing. This will help doctors understand how specific microbes in the colon affect nearby colon cells. As in the case of colibactin, the microbiome could hold important clues about colon health and tumor development.
