Is 5.7 High? Understanding the Threshold for Prediabetes

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

An A1C of 5.7% marks the entry point of prediabetes, signaling mildly elevated blood sugar that increases your risk of developing type 2 diabetes, heart disease, and related complications—often without noticeable symptoms. Prediabetes is a critical, reversible window where targeted lifestyle changes (diet, exercise, weight management) can delay or fully prevent progression to diabetes.

Explanation

An A1C test measures your average blood sugar over the past 2–3 months. If you've received a result of 5.7%, you may be wondering, "Is 5.7 high?" Here's what that number means, why it matters, and what you can do next.

What Is the A1C Test?

Also called hemoglobin A1C or HbA1c

Reflects the percentage of hemoglobin (a protein in red blood cells) that has glucose attached

Gives an overview of long-term blood sugar levels—unlike a single blood sugar reading that shows only the moment of testing

Recommended by professional groups such as the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)

A1C Categories and What They Mean

According to ADA guidelines:

Normal: below 5.7%

Prediabetes: 5.7% to 6.4%

Diabetes: 6.5% or higher (confirmed on two separate tests)

At 5.7%, you are right at the lower boundary of the prediabetes range.

A1C 5.7 Meaning

When your A1C is exactly 5.7%:

You have early, mild elevations in blood sugar.

You do not yet meet the criteria for diabetes.

You are at higher risk for progressing to type 2 diabetes if no changes are made.

Why this matters:

Prediabetes often has no obvious symptoms.

Nearly 1 in 3 U.S. adults have prediabetes—and many don't know it.

Without intervention, about 5–10% of people with prediabetes develop type 2 diabetes each year.

Why Prediabetes Matters

Left unchecked, rising blood sugar can:

Next Steps: Lifestyle Changes

Even if you're feeling fine, a 5.7% A1C calls for proactive steps:

Increase Physical Activity

Aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate exercise per week (e.g., brisk walking, cycling).

[Act Before It Too](https://pigmentlab.jcesqc8.com/)

Include strength training 2–3 times weekly to boost muscle glucose uptake.

Adopt a Balanced Diet

Focus on whole grains, lean proteins, fruits, vegetables, and healthy fats.

Limit sugary drinks, refined carbs (white bread, pastries), and highly processed snacks.

Consider small, frequent meals to stabilize blood sugar.

Lose Excess Weight

Even a 5–7% reduction in body weight (e.g., 10 pounds for a 200-pound person) can lower diabetes risk significantly.

Combine diet changes with regular activity for best results.

Manage Stress

High stress can raise blood sugar.

Practice relaxation techniques such as deep breathing, meditation, or yoga.

Improve Sleep

Aim for 7–9 hours of quality sleep each night.

Poor sleep is linked to higher blood sugar and insulin resistance.

Monitoring and Follow-Up

Retest your A1C every 3–6 months to gauge progress.

Keep track of any symptoms: increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision.

Maintain regular check-ins with your healthcare provider.

If lifestyle efforts aren't enough, your doctor may discuss medication options that can further reduce the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes.

When to Seek Professional Help

Although prediabetes itself typically isn't an emergency, you should speak to a doctor if you experience:

Sudden, unexplained weight loss

Severe fatigue or weakness

Unusual thirst or hunger

Frequent infections or slow-healing sores

Any signs of diabetic complications
