# Jaylen Brown vs. the NBA, Nike, and the World

IT'S A FEW WEEKS before the start of the NBA season, and Jaylen Brown is dripping on the Celtics’ practice court in Boston, gutting out one of his infamous four-a-day training sessions. The 28-year-old, who’s now in his ninth season, is the prototypical NBA guard, listed at 6′6″ and 223 pounds, but longer and bigger and all sinewy muscle in person. He takes two dribbles to the left, sizing up an oncoming defender (extra-equipped with two foam arm pads to be more of a shot-blocking threat), then firing up a floater for a swish. Over the next few hours, he’ll battle these defenders while launching more floaters, runners, and jump shots. Then he’ll deliver kicks and punches during an ultra-intense Muay Thai sparring session. After that, he’ll work through classic muscle-building exercises, including an isometric Nordic curl, in which he holds his entire body parallel to the floor using only the strength of his hamstrings for eight seconds. Finally, he’ll hit the pool with 100-pound weights, doing farmer’s walks and split squats with his entire body submerged.

It’s a mix of training modalities that has layered so much new muscle onto his shoulders and back that some on X think Brown is on a revenge tour this season. It’s been a wild few months for Brown, who is entering the second year of a $300 million-plus five-year contract. His performance in the NBA Finals earlier this year elevated his status as one of the league’s new generation of stars. When his much-celebrated Celtics teammate Jayson Tatum sputtered, shooting just 12 of 38 in the first two games, Brown scored 43 points on only 27 shots, pushing the Celtics to a 2–0 lead. He was also the Celtics’ best perimeter defender and was instrumental in harassing Mavs star Luka Dončić into 4.6 turnovers per game in the Finals.

Brown’s performances drew raves, including from Mavericks head coach Jason Kidd, who said Brown was Boston’s best player. Many viewed Kidd’s comment as an attempt to divide Brown and Tatum during the series. Brown does not. “If you ask him, I think he meant exactly what he was saying,” says Brown. “I think he honestly believed that was just his opinion.” The Celtics won the series 4–1, with Brown’s performance earning him the Finals MVP award. “I’ve always had the understanding of a process, of a team, and I never really complained about it. Have I thought I deserved more at certain points in my career? One thousand percent.”

In Brown’s mind, he’s the solution to any Celtics discord. “As the leader of this team,” he says, “it’s my job to bring the best out of everybody. Sometimes it’s okay to take a back seat in order for other people to shine.” His pride in being the ultimate team player made Brown’s snub from the 2024 USA Olympic basketball team surprising. Brown called out the team’s sponsor Nike on X, writing, “@nike this what we doing?” It appeared that he believed Nike influenced the decision to exclude him because he was not a Nike athlete.

Not that Brown had a quiet summer. Frustrated with the big shoe companies’ treatment of athletes, he launched his own brand, 741, and released his own signature shoe, the Rover. He continued his beef with Nike while rapping alongside A$AP Ferg on “Just Do It,” a track with more than a million Spotify plays and Brown’s baritone voice spitting, “Balling ain’t the only thing I know.” He also did another year of his five-day Bridge education camp for students of color, hosted by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. (More on that later.) He founded Boston XChange, a nonprofit that supports the growth of local Black entrepreneurs and start-ups. And he took on the role of chief innovation officer for Hapbee, a wellness tech company.

That’s a lot of extracurriculars, but Brown is building a more-than-an-athlete legacy, one that won’t allow society to box him in. He’s pursuing cultural and societal impact as well as athletic dominance. “Quick, athletic, strong…the way our society is, it just wants to shape those qualities into entertainment,” he says. “But influence [doesn’t work like that]. Influence, to me, is beyond just entertainment.” Brown is quietly confident and tactical during our interview, silent for several seconds before delivering a lengthy response to every question. He’s eager to get his points across; he makes probing eye contact to gauge if you’re vibing with his message.

His goals stretch decades beyond his playing career. What he really wants is a legacy (his word) that emulates those of his heroes. He tells me his Big Three are the Celtics great Bill Russell, Muhammad Ali, and Kobe Bryant. “Bill Russell, for what he spoke towards in terms of inequalities and justice for people of color,” he says. “Muhammad Ali, for his belief system, his faith, his charisma, his fearlessness—and his affirmation that ‘I’m the greatest.’ Kobe had that I-don’t-give-a-fuck attitude. Kobe’s confidence was real fulfilling, like he really thought that highly of himself—but also because he worked hard.”

Brown says this (and everything) completely straight-faced, daring to not only invoke the names of the giants, but to say he ranks up there with them. And yes, it seems premature, given the résumés of Russell, Ali, and Bryant, inside and outside the arena. But that’s what makes Brown’s journey compelling: Can he lead the Celtics to another championship this season (they’re favorites) while also juggling his extracurriculars? Like all of us, Brown wants to be the best version of himself, and he’s working hard to transform his body and his game. But his story is about more than self-actualization, because he’s also taking on behemoth corporations and systemic injustice. Making an impact in those arenas will be his greatest challenge.

IT STARTS WITH LEARNING

WHEN BROWN PONDERS the big problems that need solving and where he feels he can have an impact, he gravitates toward education. He calls the current system an “aggressive” way of holding children back from being successful. “Where you go to school can determine the education you get,” he says. “In America, you pay for your education. So if you don’t have money to afford it, you don’t have a contribution to society. It limits your opportunities significantly.”

Brown came to this realization long before he had the influence that’s come with his athleticism, says his mother, Mechalle Varnado-Brown, a former marketing communications professor. Brown wanted to create an education camp for kids even before he was drafted by Boston in 2016. “When he declared for the draft at UC Berkeley, he talked about ‘I want to use my platform to help others,’” she says. “He knew it would be some form of education, because that’s what, you know, gives people the freedom to be able to be successful.”

In 2018, at age 21, Brown was invited to lecture at Harvard on education, race, and institutionalized sport as part of its Askwith Education Forum. He riffed on the importance of athletes using their voices for positive change, taking issue with Fox News host Laura Ingraham’s comment that LeBron James should “shut up and dribble.” In 2019, when he was still coming off the bench for the Celtics, the MIT Media Lab named him to its Director’s Fellows program, which aimed to bring a diverse group of leaders into the organization’s culture. Brown quickly spun that into a yearly five-day camp for Black and Brown high school teenagers, where they can learn and think creatively about science, technology, engineering, art, and mathematics. His mom runs the program through his 7uice Foundation.

It was also an early chance to start carving a legacy around more than hoops. Typically, when an athlete wants to “give back,” they start a sports camp. Brown’s Bridge Program has had a different impact, setting students up to learn from astronauts and scientists of color, giving them instant proof that they can be more than athletes. “Every other player does basketball camps,” Brown says. “I do learning camps. Having someone like myself to just be around and tell [kids], ‘Yo, society is betting on you to lose. Well, I’m betting on you to win.’”

Brown knows about people betting on him to lose from his days at Wheeler High School in Marietta, Georgia. He spent his early school years in gifted programs and found himself taking high school classes while in middle school. By age 17, he had enough credits to head to college; he returned to Wheeler as a senior purely because he hadn’t won a state basketball championship.

But none of that mattered in 2014, when a teacher said to Brown, then a Wheeler senior, that she’d look him up in five years at the Cobb County Jail. And while Brown proved her wrong, he knows basketball was key. “If I didn’t have sports as that vehicle,” he says, “I wouldn’t be here. Not saying I would be far off, because I come from a strong family that’s big on education. I just wouldn’t be here.”

He pauses. He says he does none of this for attention, but he also understands the power of his own story. If he only played sports—or only ran a sports camp—he might perpetuate the idea that Black and Brown athletes have to depend on their athleticism for success. Can’t they be moguls and execs and astronauts and scientists, too? “They think, This could be me,” says Varnado-Brown of the kids in the Bridge Program. “And that gives them a whole new way to think.”

To Brown, such moments of awakening are key to marginalized communities pushing beyond their horizons. He recalls his own such awakening seven years ago, when he was still a Celtics benchwarmer in 2017. That’s when a close friend and former high school teammate, YouTuber Trevin Steede, committed suicide. The death stunned Brown, who would spend the next few months reading and studying. “That led me to a lot of rabbit holes and learning about stuff,” he says. “But it was basically, you know, I really got more into astrology and learning in astronomy and learning about the stars.” Brown pauses. He knows how this can sound, knows he mentioned astrology and astronomy in one breath. “I don’t know how to explain it any other way without it sounding crazy.”

Steede’s death motivated him to pursue his passions. And those passions almost always push you to think differently, even if it takes time to ripple out. In August 2024, he teamed up with Jason Kidd (who, like Brown, attended UC Berkeley) to launch Boston XChange to create opportunities for people of color. Brown and Kidd hope to generate $5 billion in generational wealth for communities of color. The XChange started by offering a handful of entrepreneurs up to $100,000 in grant funding for three years; it also offered them workplace and business services valued at more than $150,000. All of this, Brown hopes, will give creators of color more opportunity to grow their businesses.

“I’ve seen my body get CHALLENGED in new ways by MUAY THAI that normal training can’t do. I’m starting to move more into my MASCULINITY, more into, like, my PHYSIQUE. My body is PRIMING.”

“Here in Boston, the wealth disparity is extremely distorted, and it’s been created that way systemically,” he says. “To be able to use my resources and people who also care about this to redirect and change the city is a passion of mine.”

The trendy word to describe somebody moving and shaking like Brown, especially in tech-rich Boston, would be “disrupter.” But Brown actually bristles at this tag when it’s mentioned. “No,” he says. “I’m solutions-based. Disruption is a part of the process of solution. But everything I do is a solution to what I view as a problem.”

MASTERING HIS GAME

BROWN BRINGS THIS same problem-solving approach to his physical training. During the 2024 offseason, he doubled down on his fitness, especially the water work and Muay Thai training—both are newish pieces in his regimen. Brown first worked with famed water-training innovator and legendary big-wave surfer Laird Hamilton and his wife, Gabrielle Reece, three years ago; they helped him develop his stamina with grueling underwater strength sessions and breath work. This offseason, he did intense underwater training sessions in Bahrain with Luca Padua, also a big-wave surfer and one of Hamilton’s protégés. Now he does underwater drills with his trainer, Drew Moore, a few times a week.

His Muay Thai journey started after the Celtics were stunned by the underdog Miami Heat in the 2023 Eastern Conference Finals. In an effort to enhance his footwork, he reached out to Lionel Young, a fight trainer. Young suggested that he try Muay Thai, pointing out that it would help Brown’s hip mobility. In every offseason since then, Brown has worked with Young about three times a week. Today he’s throwing a mix of roundhouse kicks and quick punches. Barely 10 minutes in, he’s drenched. “I’ve seen my body get challenged in new ways that normal training can’t do,” he says. “I think I’m moving towards my prime. I’m starting to move more into my masculinity, more into, like, my physique. My body is priming. Nothing else can really throw me off outside of that.”

Part of that priming is wearing sneakers from his own self-funded brand, 741. Its first offering, the $200 Rover, is now available for preorder. It’s an eye-catching shoe with an all-black knit exterior made of abrasion-resistant yarn and several colored metallic drops. Brown helped design it and wore the Rover throughout the preseason, and he’s now wearing it for games. After his workout, Brown hits the lunchroom of the Celtics’ Auerbach Center to fuel up on salmon and to try not to talk about his beef with Nike.

The Rover is a sneaker born of both entrepreneurial spirit and frustration. Since leaving Adidas in 2021, Brown hasn’t worked with a sneaker brand. Often he’s played in Nikes with the Swoosh removed. The reigning NBA Finals MVP says he’s negotiated with “every brand” but has passed on “probably millions of dollars worth of deals” because none would let him actively design his signature shoe. “They wasted my time,” he says of every company from Under Armour to Puma to (yes) Nike. “They pretended that they would listen. Once it gets to the contract, they’re like, ‘Well, our superstars that we have here that are—that at the time were bigger than I was—this is the contract they have.’”

And instead of following in the footsteps of athletes who play by corporate rules (think LeBron saying, “It’s family when you talk about Nike,” and Jordan reminding us that Republicans buy shoes, too), Brown started 741. He says the name means something to him: The 7 symbolizes spiritual awakening, the 4 is about hard work, and the 1 represents new beginnings.

The brand is Brown’s most aggressive attempt to prove that his influence extends beyond sports—and it draws on an idea that Kobe Bryant was hatching shortly before his death. After 17 years with Nike, Bryant had reportedly planned to split from the Swoosh and launch Mamba, a player-owned shoe brand. Brown hopes 741 can realize that plan and eventually grow into a force that can sign other players to the shoe deals he was never offered—and the shoe deals he says players want. It’s a bold vision, but it remains to be seen whether Brown truly wants to sign other athletes—or do anything beyond making his own shoe.

Then again, even daring to make his own shoe is progress in the battle against the big footwear brands. And while it’s hard to see an athlete with a $300 million contract as an underdog, he is posting up to sneaker brands that are billion-dollar entities.

As a vice president of the National Basketball Players Association (NBPA), Brown regularly speaks to current and former NBA players—and some have complained to him about their restrictive shoe deals, which he says often bar players from wearing other brands in public, even after the brands have shelved those players’ shoes. Brown has “the respect of his peers, especially the young players,” says Andre Iguodala, the executive director of the NBPA. “He’s a leader of this generation to guide the next. That’s where he’s very impactful with leading this new group of guys, and they’ve been very receptive to all the knowledge that he’s trying to pass off to them.”

But success in the sneaker game without big-brand backing isn’t easy. Stephon Marbury, who played for five NBA teams in the 2000s, briefly had a budget shoe line with Steve and Barry’s. Kyrie Irving, a close friend of Brown’s, was let go by Nike in 2022 and signed a five-year deal last year with the Chinese sportswear company ANTA. He’s its flagship athlete, although his shoes aren’t exactly on blacktops around the country.

Brown doesn’t care about those limitations. “To me, the whole ‘If you’re Nike, you’ve got to wear only Nike or Pumaʼ—it’s like there need to be some more options,” he says. “It’s kind of boring. The same designs. I’d love to see new brands emerge that are represented, owned, by athletes. If I can spark that, great.”

Even if that means more Nike hate. He declines to elaborate on the rumor that he was left off this year’s Olympic team because of his perceived beef with Nike. (MH reached out to Nike for a comment but did not hear back.) But Brown does say many players fear backlash. “They know how the system is, and they don’t want to get outcasted if you stand up against the way things are done,” he says. “I don’t have any fear about any of that.”

Iguodala strikes a note of caution and respect. “Obviously we know the Jim Browns, the Muhammad Alis, the [Colin] Kaepernicks—those who have suffered financially or whose careers have been cut short because they’ve been outspoken. I’m not putting Jaylen in that bucket, but you can say this is something that’s similar. I’ve just been proud of him for how he stayed true to who he is.”

EXTENDING HIS INFLUENCE

IF YOU WATCHED the NBA Finals, you may have noticed Brown wearing what looked like a black plastic necklace pregame and during his press conferences. It’s a device made by a Canadian wellness wearables company called Hapbee Technologies, and Brown is its chief innovation officer. Prior to his training session, Brown invited me to join him for part of a four-hour workshop with eight people from Hapbee’s leadership team in the ninth-floor conference room of a skyscraper in downtown Boston.

[the NBA Nike and](https://collagen-atelier.bbsxyxy.com/blog/3930498386.html)

Riz Shah, Hapbee’s chief commercial officer, says he connected with Brown over his curiosity about technology and desire to “build a company with soul.” “Hapbee needs to have a purpose,” says Shah. “We’re out there to help people. This phrase about helping people be the best version of themselves, that’s Jaylen. That’s the leadership, and then making sure that everyone buys in. J was pushing us to, like, be more, have sort of the effects—that the visual effects be more engaging. Hapbee being more elegant in design. He’s really into details.”

The company’s signature tech is called frequency therapy, and it’s being developed for medical uses by neurobiologist Xavier A. Figueroa, PhD, an affiliate assistant professor at the University of Washington. Magnetic coils deliver energy waves to your nerve cells to stimulate various emotional states, such as focused (mimicking an effect of nicotine), alert (caffeine), happy (alcohol), and calm (THC), as well as deep sleep (melatonin). There are currently 75 signals and signal combinations, and the waves are supposed to act on the brain at a cellular level. The coils live within the necklace, or within the housing of Hapbee’s recently released $200 sleep pad.

Clinical research on the devices is ongoing, and MH’s brain health advisor, P. Murali Doraiswamy, MD, a professor at the Duke University Institute for Brain Sciences, notes that while there is evidence that this kind of therapy can increase brain activity, it’s harder to pinpoint specific benefits, such as healing or mood enhancement. He adds that the placebo effect may produce a benefit as well, typically seen in 30 percent of people. Still, the therapy is gaining popularity with biohackers like Dave Asprey, who is an investor in the company. “The Hapbee wearable is my go-to piece of tech for modifying my mental state,” Asprey gushed on his podcast. “Want more energy? Dial it up. More rest? Done. Same thing for focus, sleep, stress.” And Target recently started stocking the sleep pad.

Hapbee and Brown hope this leadership meeting can help develop a strategy to fast-track mainstream acceptance, and a PowerPoint presentation from chief science officer Brian Mogen, PhD, breaks down the device’s inner workings. Brown scrawls notes on a white legal pad during the presentation—then looks up. Brown offers a compelling and memorable catchphrase for the product (but we can’t reveal it because of a nondisclosure agreement). Heads nod. The company’s been looking for a sexy, accessible description for its science. This may be it. Brown picked up the CIO role last spring, when he invested in the company. He made that investment partly because he was offered an active role in device design. “I’m a designer,” he says. “I designed my shoes, I’ve designed clothes, I design curriculums. Once they heard about my design résumé, they thought it was fitting that I would be responsible for designing the next iterations of the devices for the next 5 to 10 years.”

“The players know how the SYSTEM is, and they don’t want to get OUTCASTED if you STAND UP against the way things are done. I don’t have any FEAR about any of that.”

If Hapbee is successful, Brown will extend his influence into the wellness world, following in the footsteps of crossover influencers like Gwyneth Paltrow and Tom Brady. Not that Hapbee has created major buzz yet. The company’s market cap is currently $9.81 million, and it seems most likely to break through as a maker of sleep aids. Brown did tell me that he naps for nearly two hours daily—all that training, all those meetings. Maybe the device helps with that. And in a small but powerful way, it’s helping him be that better version of himself. “I noticed having even more brain capacity to focus even longer and retain more information,” he says. “Naps are, like, lifesaving.”

And all the things that Brown does, from the NBA titles he’s seeking to the money he’s raising for the underprivileged to the programs he’s setting up, are part of his legacy. He refuses to choose between dominance on the court and impact off it. He’ll make his mark in both worlds.

“I don’t think we have to separate,” he says. “It’s just Jaylen as a human being. What solutions did he create? That’s how I want to be viewed. I would like for my legacy to be, in 10 years or 20 years from now, that we look at Jaylen Brown as a pioneer for how athletes look at themselves and understand the value they have.”

No Nike deal needed.
