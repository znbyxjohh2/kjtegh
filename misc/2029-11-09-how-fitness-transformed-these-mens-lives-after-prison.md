# How Fitness Transformed These Mens' Lives After Prison

This story is part of Black in Fitness, a series of articles highlighting the challenges and triumphs faced by Black trainers, athletes, and gym owners within the fitness community from their own perspective. Read the rest of the stories here.

JOE SEELY, 40, is a personal trainer who splits time between New York City and Los Angeles and Jay Jordan, 33, is a personal trainer based in Atlanta. Both have participated in the Men's Health/Women's Health Strength in Diversity Initiative. Seeley and Jordan also both spent time in prison before embarking upon successful careers in the fitness industry.

Parts of their experience are all too common. Black Americans are incarcerated at a disproportionate rate; according to statistics from the Prison Policy Initiative, 37 percent of Americans who are incarcerated in jails or prisons are Black, despite being only 13 percent of the overall population. The likelihood of building a stable life after a sentence can be precarious, too. Up to 60 percent of formerly incarcerated people could be jobless, according to a different Prison Policy Initiative report.

Seeley and Jordan, however, found a path forward after their sentences through fitness. “Everything I'm doing now, I wrote down in prison—but I never knew it was gonna come like it's coming,” says Jordan. “A while back I posted my journal I had in prison. I did everything step by step.” Along with everyday clients, Jordan has trained pro athletes like boxing champions Robert Easter and Devon Alexander, while Seeley has trained music superstar Megan Thee Stallion.

Last month, the pair sat down with Men's Health senior editor Keith Nelson for a conversation about their experiences, their hopes for the future, and the path forward they hope others going through similar experiences can follow.

Men's Health: How did you guys get into fitness?

JOE SEELEY: When I came home in 2016 , they sent me to see the psych—it was a court order from the judge. [The psych] said, “You’ve got to get a job at Fresh Direct.” I said “Yo, if I go work at Fresh Direct, y’all lock me the fuck up.” I started seeing people going to the gym. I’m watching like, “I can do this shit.” A lady that worked there had known me from Riker’s Island from back in the day. She said “You need a job?” I started working at the front desk and never looked back. Of course when you’re first coming up you're doing bullshit, you’re late to sessions—but you're learning. There’s a bunch of shit that our community needs to know. They think they just call Legal Zoom and open up a fucking LLC and call it ‘This Fitness.’ When people see me, I want them to know that this shit was a grind.

JAY JORDAN: When I was going through my legal troubles, I already had in my mind “I'm going to work out.” It was 2013. When I got to prison, it was just the workout thing. That's what helped me with my time, helped me stay focused. While I was in there I was still selling drugs, dealing, doing crazy stuff. But my mind was on working out.

[Lives After](https://pepvoice.com/)

I met this guy out of Columbus and he was finishing up 10 years, Odulex Ledger. So we got together and we started working out. An incident happened on the yard where I almost got caught with drugs. I woke up in the morning, and I ended up giving it all away. I ended up doing my research on a few things and I came up with my name, Design A Body, sent it home, got the LLC for it. I got my logo and everything done while I was in prison. I was about two months away from going to the halfway house and I was telling Lex, “I ain’t got it man.” He was like “Jay I’m telling you like, you gonna be great out there.” And I'm like, “Man, you’ve been leading the workouts. I don't know what I'm gonna do when I get out there.” Because I'm still thinking about getting out—like reality. This ain’t behind the walls no more. Moving fast. But when I got out, being in the halfway house, it was slow. I started getting back into that mindset of getting fast money. A guy I was in prison with ended up coming to pick me up just to get me out the halfway house one day, and he took me to the gym. I started training there in 2017 and it was history. I met Robert Easter, he was a professional boxer [and former IBF champion]. And he's like, “I want you as my trainer” because he saw me consistently working out. We did a camp, won a fight. Then I started working with [IBF/WBC champion boxer] Devon Alexander, and I did [former NFL player] Pacman Jones’ celebrity fight. Just kept on.

Joe, did you have a training or a fitness mindset while you were in prison?

SEELEY: I was doing bullshit, because it’s like that when you're doing time. When you’re doing time you’re not thinking about no outdate, you’re just trying to make it to the next commissary, to the next phone call. I had lost my phone because some gang shit had happened. So then they had us locked up for 23 and one [solitary confinement], I was gone for a minute. When I got back I was just reading shit. I actually took a personal training cert in the Fed, but come to find out that shit wasn't shit when I got out. I just always had the mindset that I’m just gonna work out because my next charge is 15 to life. Times is changing. We’re getting older, I’m 40. So I don't have no more time to be giving these people.

JORDAN: When you [have success], it’s just giving the people behind them walls or the people that come home hope. I get a lot of DMs from people that are still incarcerated. Like, “Man, I'm gonna be doing the same thing you're doing when I get home.” Sometimes I just lay in the bed like, “Man, I'm changing some people's lives.”

SEELEY: It's like, yeah, we’re doing it for our lifestyle. We’re doing it to change lives. I didn't have nobody to look up to and be like, “Yo, come do this, let’s do this positive.” Now I see I’ve gotta be here for my kids, or I’ve got to be here for my woman. So like you said, when people do hit us up, like, “I’m about to do this, what cert do I take?” I'm leading in the right direction.

[Mens' Lives After Prison](https://saltbbs.com/wph6mq8u/)

JORDAN: I like what he said, because people reach out to me and I send them in the right direction. And they'll be like, “Man, thank you so much.” And I'll be like, "Man, I'm just doing what somebody else didn't do for me when I came home.”

So you didn’t have a person who showed you how to be a Black man in fitness?

JORDAN: I've been home six years now. These six years I did it on my own. From the end of 2017 one way, going to the halfway house to now. There’s a lot of people looking at it like, you got where you at now. But they don't know the footwork I had to put in. This is me—I'm spending my hard-earned money to go out of town and meet this person, and go network with this person. That’s the footwork. But I was still in a prison mindset. Everything is still structured.

When you guys go through life and you go through your careers and everything, do you see yourself as a Black trainer or a trainer?

SEELEY: I just see myself as a Black man that changed everything negative to positive. I came in the commercial gym, New York Sports Club. The dude there was Pierre, he did some time. He basically showed me the game. But some people, they're not going to show you everything. You’ve got to learn yourself. Me, I just want to say I’m a trainer. Because they try to put stipulations on that Black shit. So I'm a trainer for my hood.

JORDAN: I don't do all the “I’m a Black trainer.” I love my people. It’s Black excellence for sure. But I'm just the trainer that changed my life around.

Have you ever been inside a room where you might have been the only Black guy there?

SEELEY: Sometimes, especially with my tattoos. I’ve got 70-year-old clients, 80-year-old clients. They invite me to their house for dinner, the whole family’s there. So you’ve got to be comfortable with who you are. I know what I've been through. But like I said, I’m just a trainer.

JORDAN: I think the story of you changing your life around, it builds a relationship with your clients. Because, like how he said, he’s got clients that invite him to the house. Most of my clients, I go to their house to train, and they trust me. You're gonna judge me or you're not.

When you guys are in those rooms where maybe you have white clients, when you're the only Black guy in the room, how do you navigate that?

SEELEY: You’ve got to be yourself. I'm gonna talk to my clients how I talk. I'm just gonna tone it down a little bit and talk more professional but I want to be me. I'm not doing crazy shit because I still got kids looking up to me. Like, my pops was doing time. My pops did 25 to life. So I didn't have a father figure. My daughter’s running track, so I need her to look up to me. When she comes to see me, she sees me working.

JORDAN: For me, it’s my daughter—everything she sees about me is work.

Are there any preconceived notions or assumptions you've felt from people for being an ex-convict and a Black man? Sometimes I'll go into an interview, and the person won't know that I'm Black and they go, "Oh." Even something small like that?

JORDAN: I'm not gonna say the name, but a trainer, celebrity influencer, or whatever you want to call it—the guy was like, “You need to take that prison stuff off your (Instagram) page.” I ain’t taking this off my page, that’s my journey. I’m not taking it down for one person, because there’s 100 other people out there that want me and want to hear my story.

SEELEY: Some clients, they figure it out, like “Oh my gosh, you were locked up?” So I'll go like, “Yeah, whatever.” You gotta get to know me. I don't talk about old shit. This my first time really sitting down talking about this shit. But I don't mind telling my part if it could guide somebody else to be a better man. So I’mma be me, and when you see me now, you see me nine times of 10 I'm in sweats, ready to work.

JORDAN: Us, coming from where we’re coming from, as a trainer, you’re always in go-mode. You always want to be comfortable.

SEELEY: You’re up at four in the morning and training, and you still got to work yourself out. You still got to deal with your personal issues. So it's like just being a Black man, period, is dealing with this. So it's like you said with the Black man shit: It's just part of just being a Black man. You know, we’re built Ford tough.

So what is success to you guys?

SEELEY: I've been at it to where I was doing everything negative. So wealth to me is just making sure I got some clean sweats. My bills is paid, and I'm able to do what the fuck I want to do every day. I try not to get caught up with Instagram. I don't want to chase away my regular clients. I’ve got a base of clients that helped me be who I am today.

[Mens' Lives After Prison](https://pepenails.com/y4akav/)

JORDAN: Success for me is a billion-dollar success—but I feel like I'm successful already. I feel like I was successful when I figured out I changed my life around.

What do you think is the biggest challenge for people of color in the fitness industry?

JORDAN: Clout.

SEELEY: Yeah, clout. Everybody wants to be an influencer, nobody wants to get certified.

JORDAN: And then a lot of people still don't know, even when you get to where you want to be, or where you feel like you want to be, it's still a grind. I'm still grinding. I know how to capitalize off of it.

SEELEY: So that being said, it's not even about the money no more. It's about the connections. You can help this one person, and this one person can take you to the fucking moon. We burned bridges coming up. So now that we’re here, it's not even about burning bridges, it’s about the connections.

[Transformed These Mens'](https://3899241.com/7zzbyiu/)

How many of your early clients were Black people?

SEELEY: Not a lot for me.

[How Fitness Transformed](https://mindpeptide.bbsxyxy.com/)

JORDAN: In Ohio, I had a white family. They were consistent. But outside of that, it was my people that love what I was doing, that love my story. I inspire other people off my story, so that's why I'm not shy from sharing.

SEELEY: I gotta get used to it.

JORDAN: It's the journey. People want to hear it. For real, people tell me all the time “I wouldn’t have thought you went to jail.” They’ll be like “I wouldn’t have thought you did four and a half years.” I'm like, “I did every day.”

What do you guys want to see more of from Black people with fitness going into the future?

JORDAN: I just want to see more of trainers being themselves.

SEELEY: Be yourself. Facts.

How do you feel the Black community has helped your career?

JORDAN: They know how Black men can get out [from prison], coming from my situation, and going back into doing what they used to. Me doing something positive now—you go back to saying Black excellence. I think that's a big part of it. Coming from a situation to where I'm at now, a lot of Black people like that, it’s that you're not a statistic.

SEELEY: We’re strong people, period. Sometimes you’ve got to leave out of the community to come back and help the community. Me just being Black, and being where I’m from it’s like a different type of drive. And this just gives me the fire every day to get out and do shit positive and keep learning.
