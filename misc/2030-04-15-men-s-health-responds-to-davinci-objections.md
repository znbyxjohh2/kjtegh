# Men's Health Responds to DaVinci Objections

On May 5th, we received a letter from Dave Rosa, senior vice president of scientific affairs for Intuitive Surgical, maker of the DaVinci surgical robot. He was responding to the Men's Health feature "What's Wrong with Robotic Surgery?" by Laura Beil, in the May issue of the magazine. The letter, which contained several points the Intuitive team took exception to, is reproduced here.

We pride ourselves on the accuracy of our reporting at Men's Health, so we took Intuitive's request for corrections very seriously. After an internal investigation—with the cooperation of Laura Beil and our research team, led by Jaclyn Colletti—we stand behind the article in its entirety, as published.

Here is our response to Intuitive.

Dear Mr. Rosa—

I am the editor of Men's Health magazine, and Laura Beil's direct editor. She has shared your concerns, which I have also discussed with our research department. Laura consistently produces thoroughly reported articles on complex topics. In fact, a recent article in the JAMA Internal Medicine specifically cited the accuracy and balance of her reporting. I invite you to read it: A Guide to Reading Health Care News Stories

After weighing each of your points, I stand by her work on this story.

More specifically:

Objection 1

[Men's Health Responds](https://cortexcompass.jcesqc8.com/)

• You write: "It does sound promising: precision machines displacing fallible human beings, operating in the most sensitive areas."

This is wrong. The da Vinci Surgical System does not "displace fallible human beings." The robotic system is a tool used by surgeons. Surgeons, nurses and other operating room attendants are not displaced by the da Vinci. Please correct.

The article described how the surgical system works. In addition, many paragraphs were dedicated to the role of a surgeon's skill in positive patient outcomes. It would, in fact, be quite impossible to read the story and not know that a surgeon was involved, and not understand that the phrase "displace fallible human beings" referenced human shortcomings, not human beings altogether. In fact, your company's slogan is "Taking surgery beyond the limits of the human hand." Are you not making the same point in your marketing materials that we did, editorially?

Objection 2

[DaVinci Objections](https://clinical.peppotom.com/article/natriuretic-peptide-receptor-c-the-frontier-research-potential-of-natriuretic-peptide-receptor-c-in-modern-academics-peptide-share-b7eaca47a826/)

• You quote an expert: "Many of these studies are weak in quality," Schabowsky says. "They also lack consistency, and the evidence isn't necessarily strong enough to determine whether using this device is better or worse.''

[Men's Health Responds to](https://tiancansz.com/bcjjnc0/)

[Responds to DaVinci Objections](https://ppmaas.com/9ubpvt7kn9/)

The truth: Many are NOT weak in quality. Schabowsky's group, ECRI, requires a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to consider the evidence to be strong, which is an easy, but very incorrect, criterium to apply. RCTs in surgery generally are weaker studies than large population-based studies because they are done at a single institution with large operator effect, rather than measuring directly the result of applying a new therapy to an entire population. No less an authority than Sir Michael Rawlings of NICE and the general board of the lnternational Health Technology Assessment Association have decried this desire for RCTs in device studies and recommended post-market observational registry studies to be the gold standard. Schabowsky is using out-of-date criteria. Better quality studies do exist, and they do support improved da Vinci outcomes.

The ECRI Institute is a non-profit, highly regarded institution, and a source of impartial information about medical devices. They are federally designated as an "evidence-based practice center," with the mission of evaluating the scientific literature as a whole when it comes to safety, quality and effectiveness in matters of healthcare. You are free to disagree with the institute's conclusions, but it is appropriate for us as journalists to consult one of the few highly credible, unbiased sources on medical devices.

Objection 3

[Men's Health Responds](https://thymosin-report.hbsnzj.com/)

• You write: "Lawyers tried unsuccessfully to persuade the jury that the company knew the doctor's skills were lacking but pressured him to use it anyway. (The case is on appeal.)

While this is true, you made a glaring omission. The argument was unsuccessful because the doctor's own testimony said da Vinci representatives tried to persuade him NOT to do the case. Such an omission suggests that you were not interested in the facts if they did not fit your preconceived theme.

Only members of the jury know exactly why the case was lost, and it was no quick decision. Jurors took a day and half to reach a verdict after five weeks of testimony. It is true that the company urged the doctor to start with simple cases, and instead his first patient was obese. But the surgeon also testified that he was made to feel like he was adequately trained to use the system after two practice runs, when in retrospect he was not. (See Intuitive's First Robot-Surgery Trial to Probe Training)

Intuitive won the case, and this was made clear in the story. Why the company won is not a judgment Men's Health can make. While you believe it was from the doctor's testimony, the lawyers for the other side have a different opinion. (We've talked to them.)

[Men's Health Responds to](https://regeneration-desk.hbsnzj.com/blog/8155448680.html)

Objection 4

[Men's Health Responds to](https://defensecascade.china-akan.com/blog/0631233323.html)

• You write: "Intuitive realized a few years ago that breaches in tip covers posed a bum threat. The company developed tougher replacement tips and in 2011 sent a reminder to hospitals about proper tip-cover care. But company officials did not feel the problem was significant enough to trouble the FDA with it. After an audit, the FDA disagreed- not only with Initiative's decision not to alert the agency to tip-cover deficiencies but also with its failure to keep the FDA in the loop on notifications it had been sending to robot owners."

This is misleading at best, and Intuitive representatives made that clear to you. The company has extensive documentation of its communications with the FDA regarding the "tip-cover deficiencies." The only thing the FDA disagreed with was not keeping them "in the loop" on certain customer notifications.

Laura checked this exact wording with Intuit's PR representative, Angela Wonson, to make sure it was correct. Ms. Wonson replied that the paragraph was accurate—not misleading, as you state. Her only objection was that "it is incorrect to state that we didn't file paperwork about safety issues. Otherwise it's correct." The reference to this being a safety matter was then revised in the text.

As for your objection that that the FDA's only complaint was not keeping them in the loop, I invite you to review the entire warning letter here. It is quite lengthy, and indicates much more serious problems. Among other things, it states that even after inspectors specifically requested information, they found your company's response "incomplete and inadequate."

Objection 5

• You quote Marty Makary, the go-to da Vinci critic for reporters: "This is exactly the story of what's wrong with American health care. The cost is passed down the line until you, the health care customer, pay it in the form of higher bills and insurance premiums.''

While that's a nice sound bite, it ignores reams of research that shows da Vinci surgery for a number of procedures reduces the overall cost of care. Again, Intuitive shared that information with you and offered to provide even more detailed information. But it's obvious that the facts did not suit your narrative.

You did send studies on cost, and Laura read them. But she also read studies you did not hand-select, such as the discussion of robotic surgery costs that that appeared in the New England Journal of Medicine, the most prestigious medical journal worldwide. And she read studies like this and this which were larger and more recent. In addition, she read an extensive review—which includes all studies conducted so far—published in February in European Urology. (You can find it here). The senior author is Dr. Jim Hu, a proponent of the da Vinci system quoted extensively in the article. But the conclusion was this: "Cost effectiveness of new technologies should be assessed before widespread adoption. To date, in the lone study to evaluate this, RALP was not found to be cost effective from a health care, economic standpoint."

In sum, I believe that this story was accurate, fair and a service to our readers. In fact, robotic surgery represents an advance in technology, but one that has physical and financial costs.
