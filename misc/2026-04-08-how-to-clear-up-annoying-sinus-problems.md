# How to Clear Up Annoying Sinus Problems

[to Clear Up](https://copperglow.dns0663.com/)

FOR AS LONG as sinuses have been around, aka forever, there’s been a ton that scientists haven’t figured out about them. We’re aware that our sinuses help our noses work better, keep our skulls lighter, and act as “crumple zones” that cushion impacts to the head. But, among other things, why must they drain through the nasal cavity, which is the root of many problems? “I don’t know that we actually know the answer,” says Jean Kim, M.D., Ph.D., a professor of otolaryngology at Johns Hopkins Medicine.

Dr. Kim and fellow experts do know that sinus issues are getting worse, and they have a few guesses as to why. For one, sterile modern life exposes us to fewer irritants, giving our bodies fewer chances to learn to deal with them. Climate change is making allergy seasons worse and irritants like wildfire smoke more common. Obesity can pressurize parts of the head that interface with the sinuses.

But that still leaves perhaps the most confusing thing of all: Often we think we have “sinus issues,” but they’re something else entirely. So how do you fix them? With a quick anatomy lesson, some insight on root causes, and various pros who can help.

What’s in There, Anyway?

"SINUS" IS A generic term that means “cavity” or “hollow space.” When you complain about “sinus issues,” you’re referring to what happens in the paranasal sinuses, four pairs of spaces that drain through your nasal cavity and down the back of your throat. Knowing what’s there helps you take care of it better.

What Sinus Trouble Is—and Isn’t

The Actual Sinus Problem: Sinusitis

[Clear Up Annoying Sinus](https://matrix-skin.xianfengchem.com/)

A CLASSIC AILMENT—what you think you have when you say, “I’m having sinus issues”—is sinusitis, aka a sinus infection. Infections in the sinuses lead to nasty mucus that drains into the nose, causing inflammation and swelling in both the sinuses and the nose. There are five key symptoms: snotty nose, postnasal drip, congestion, facial pain, and an altered sense of smell. The bad news: Those are such general symptoms that the only way to know for sure that you’ve got a sinus infection is to have an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialist scope your nose. The good news: According to John Craig, M.D., a rhinologist at Henry Ford Health, more than 90 percent of the time, a sinus infection resolves on its own in one or two weeks.

For relief until then, use saline to flush out irritants (via a saline spray or neti pot), ibuprofen or acetaminophen for fever or facial pressure, and an OTC decongestant for stuffiness. Antibiotics have fallen out of favor.

3 Symptoms You Might Attribute to Sinus Problems

1. So. Much. Facial. Pressure. You’re convinced you have a “sinus headache.”

WHAT ELSE IT COULD BE: A migraine, tension headache, or other malady related to the huge number of nerve endings in your face. “We published a study a couple of years ago: 95 percent of people that presented to my office with their most prominent symptom being ‘sinus headache’ ended up getting a neurologic diagnosis, not sinusitis,” says Dr. Craig.

KEY QUESTION: Is your mucus thick or colored? If not, it’s likely a headache or nerve issue, even if you’re snotty.

WHAT TO TRY: Acetaminophen or ibuprofen (for tension headaches) or something like Excedrin (which adds aspirin and migraine-reducing caffeine). Headaches can be tricky; see a doc if they’re frequent.

2. Runny nose. You feel stuffy and puffy.

WHAT ELSE IT COULD BE: Allergies.

KEY QUESTION: In addition to being an absolute puddle, are your nose and eyes itchy? That’s a classic indicator of allergies.

WHAT TO TRY: First aim to eliminate your exposure to allergens, then get medicine: See if an OTC oral antihistamine (e.g., Claritin, Allegra) works. And to give your nose a hand, use a saline rinse to flush out allergens, followed by a steroid nasal spray (like Flonase or Nasacort). Sprays may take three days of use to kick in but are often more effective in controlling allergy symptoms in the long run, says Dr. Craig.

3. You’re feeling the dreaded postnasal drip.

WHAT ELSE IT COULD BE: Believe it or not, a form of acid reflux called laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR).

KEY QUESTIONS: Do you have a raspy voice or a dry cough? Feel like something’s stuck in your throat?

WHAT TO TRY: With LPR, acid makes it all the way to your throat but may not cause heartburn, so you wouldn’t suspect acid reflux. Drink lots of water—it will moisturize your esophagus and act as a “replacement behavior” for clearing your throat (which can cause more irritation). You can also try proton-pump inhibitors (such as Prilosec). Better: Talk to a doc about the best ways to manage LPR through behavioral changes, including what you eat and how you sleep.

Individual symptoms have low predictive power, says Dr. Craig. A doctor’s overall impression is much more helpful, but even then, going off symptoms alone offers only a 50 to 75 percent chance of accurately diagnosing sinusitis. If you’re a mess, expect a referral to an ear, nose, and throat specialist.

Rhinologist

The absolute expert on sinus anatomy. One thing they’ll almost certainly do is put an endoscope in your nose and/or throat to get visual confirmation of an infection. A rhinologist can perform surgery, too—probably to enlarge the “drains” from your sinuses. It’s uncomfortable but low risk.

If the pattern of your symptoms suggests an allergy, they’ll help you identify the allergen, make a plan for avoiding it, and (if needed) get you on one of the many medicines that will potentially help.

Dental specialist

Some sinusitis traces back to infections of the teeth or gums. Since that’s outside the purview of an ENT or allergist, you’ll want to see a periodontist, endodontist, or oral surgeon to manage the root cause.
