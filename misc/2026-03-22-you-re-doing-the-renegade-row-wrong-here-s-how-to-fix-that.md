# You're Doing the Renegade Row Wrong. Here's How to Fix That.

VERY FEW EXERCISES have names that evoke anything more than describing the action taking place when you perform them. Think squats, curls, lunges—all helpfully explanatory, but not exciting. When you're looking for a way to train multiple muscle groups of your upper body in on shot, however, you can take on an exercise that combines two more literally-named moves into one effective maneuver that actually sounds cool: the renegade row.

﻿There are a few schools of thought when it comes to the renegade row, but all of them combine the pushup﻿ and plank row﻿. Some trainers will rush you through the pushups and row reps—avoid them at all costs. Good trainers will break up each component part deliberately so you'll get the most out of your effort.

Shoulders Directly Over Wrists

Eb says: There are two key differences between the renegade row's ideal pushup position and your standard pushup position: Setting your feet up wide, and setting your shoulders up directly over your wrists. The latter thing is extra-important, because it sets you up both for success in executing the row here, and in protecting your shoulders long-term. Place your hands too wide, and your shoulder muscles have to stabilize at an unnatural angle that compromises the joint.

[Wrong Here's How to](https://tissue-renewal.china-akan.com/blog/9683483757.html)

Also, be sure to avoid setting up with your hands in front of your shoulders. You'll commonly see this in group fitness classes when people have to do renegade rows while under fatigue, and they start to hike their butts in plank position and shift their shoulders back away from their hands. You're asking the shoulders to stabilize from a less than natural angle, so you don't want this to happen.

Row Back, Not Up

Eb says: When you start to do the row, don't focus on moving the dumbbell up in a straight line. Instead, focus on driving your elbow back toward your lower ribs. This will help you avoid over-rotating through your torso at the start of the row and make it easier to keep your hips and shoulders square to the ground (which is critical to the entire move). This will also insure that you're firing from your lats and rhomboids first on the row, instead of over-involving your biceps.

Fight to Keep Hips and Shoulders Square

Eb says: The great challenge of completing the row portion is owning the plank position and not rotating at your hips. This move is easy if you're willing to be sloppy, but if you want to really lock in your form and get better back and core development, you need to battle to keep your hips and shoulders square to the ground at all times.

That isn't easy, even with a light weight, because your body's first instinct will be to hike your hip on the rowing side to help make things easier. Avoid this by actively squeezing your glutes and flexing your obliques on the rowing side.

Thinking Heavy? Start Lighter.

Eb says: You'll see plenty of people loading up with weight on the renegade row, and you certainly can—as long as you can keep fighting to keep your hips and shoulders square. But your first obligation in the renegade row is to own and master the plank, staying flat in it at all times—and a single-arm pushup-position plank is challenging even with no weight. So start lighter and dominate that position, then progress from there.

﻿Benefits of the Renegade Row

﻿The renegade row is really two exercises in one: the pushup and the plank row (you could even argue you can squeeze a third move in, a high plank, if you really take your time between reps). You'll be able to hit multiple muscle groups in your upper body in one go, making it an extremely efficient exercise if you're looking to get the most bang for your workout buck. If you're doing the movement properly, you'll build strength, hone coordination, and even reinforce proper posture.

﻿Common Renegade Row Mistakes

The renegade row is a multi-part exercise, so there are several spots where you have to be cognizant of your form. That starts in the setup. Make sure that your hands aren't too far in front of your shoulders and your feet are wide—you'll end up putting yourself in a poor position to stay stable for the row. You'll need three points of contact to retain a stable base.

﻿Make sure that you don't rush through the exercise. The focus should be on keeping your hips and butt level—keep your core engaged and avoid rotating as you row the weight up. If you feel your torso turning, slow down.

﻿Another mistake is working with weights that are too heavy for you to handle. Drop down to lighter weights that allow you to perform the reps without compensating.

﻿How to Add the Renegade Row to Your Workouts

﻿The renegade row is a solid exercise to add to upper or full-body workouts if you're low on time or you want to vary up your routine. You won't build massive back muscle since you won't be able to work with heavy weights, but you'll still get a good bang for your buck. Start with 3 sets of 6 to 8 reps per side, making sure to keep your form strong.
