What are lutein and zeaxanthin?

Lutein and zeaxanthin are naturally occurring pigments called carotenoids, the same family of compounds responsible for the bright yellow, orange, and red colors found in foods like corn, peppers, and egg yolks. They're closely related to beta-carotene — the carotenoid that gives carrots their orange color and can be converted into vitamin A. Unlike beta-carotene, however, lutein and zeaxanthin aren't used to make vitamin A. Instead, they accumulate in specific tissues, particularly the eyes, where they perform their own unique functions.3 15

They're best known for accumulating in the macula, a small area near the center of the retina that's responsible for sharp, detailed vision. In fact, lutein, zeaxanthin, and their closely related cousin, meso-zeaxanthin, are the only carotenoids found in meaningful amounts within the macula, which is why they're often referred to collectively as the macular carotenoids.16

Most people can obtain lutein and zeaxanthin through foods like spinach, kale, egg yolks, corn, and pistachios. However, typical Western diets often provide considerably less than the amounts used in clinical studies, which is one reason these supplements have become increasingly popular.17

How do lutein and zeaxanthin work?

Lutein and zeaxanthin primarily work as antioxidants. In simple terms, they help neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) — unstable molecules that form naturally during metabolism and in response to things like sunlight and pollution.3 Left unchecked, ROS can damage cells over time through a process known as oxidative stress.18

Researchers believe this antioxidant activity explains many of the benefits associated with lutein and zeaxanthin, though the strength of the evidence varies depending on what's being studied.15 Here's where the science stands today.

Eye health and visual performance

Eye health is where lutein and zeaxanthin have by far the strongest scientific support.

In addition to acting as antioxidants, lutein and zeaxanthin perform another function that's especially important in the eye: they absorb some high-energy blue light before it reaches sensitive retinal tissue.3 19 Researchers believe these two mechanisms work together to help protect the retina from cumulative oxidative damage.

One of the most consistent findings across clinical trials is an increase in macular pigment optical density (MPOD), a measure of the protective pigments in the macula. A large 2021 meta-analysis found that supplementation consistently increased MPOD, while another systematic review linked higher MPOD with improvements in contrast sensitivity and other measures of visual function.4 20

Much of what we know about lutein and zeaxanthin comes from the landmark Age-Related Eye Disease Studies (AREDS and AREDS2), sponsored by the National Eye Institute. Researchers launched these large clinical trials to answer an important question: could nutritional supplements help slow the progression of AMD?

The original AREDS study identified a combination of nutrients that helped reduce the risk of progression to advanced AMD. Researchers later refined that formula in AREDS2 after beta-carotene, one of the original ingredients, was linked to an increased risk of lung cancer in people who smoke or formerly smoked.42 They found that replacing beta-carotene with lutein and zeaxanthin maintained the formula's benefits while offering a safer option for those populations.5 Secondary analyses also suggested lutein and zeaxanthin may have provided even greater protection against progression to advanced AMD among people with lower dietary intakes of these carotenoids.5

These improvements aren't dramatic, but they're remarkably consistent across studies, making lutein and zeaxanthin among the best-supported nutrients for maintaining healthy vision.

Digital eye strain

As screen time has increased, researchers have naturally wondered whether lutein and zeaxanthin might also help with digital eye strain.

The evidence is still developing. In one randomized controlled trial, adults who took 10mg of lutein and 2mg of zeaxanthin daily for six months experienced improvements in tear production, tear-film stability, and recovery after bright-light exposure compared with placebo. However, they didn't report significantly less eye strain or better contrast sensitivity.6

A separate 2025 study found improvements in MPOD, contrast sensitivity, sleep quality, and visual comfort among heavy screen users taking a lutein-zeaxanthin supplement.22 Together, these findings suggest lutein and zeaxanthin may help support visual comfort during prolonged screen use, but larger studies are still needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.

Cognitive health

Over the past decade, researchers have also become interested in lutein and zeaxanthin's potential role in healthy brain aging.

Several randomized trials have reported modest improvements in areas such as visual memory, learning, and neural processing speed after supplementation.7 8 However, other studies — including the cognitive arm of AREDS2 — found no significant improvements in older adults with AMD.23

Taken together, the research is promising, but still mixed. At this point, lutein and zeaxanthin appear more likely to support certain aspects of cognition than to broadly improve memory or mental performance.

Skin health

The skin, like the eyes, is constantly exposed to oxidative stress from UV radiation.

Clinical trials suggest lutein and zeaxanthin may help improve skin hydration, elasticity, and resistance to UV-induced damage after several months of supplementation.9 24 46 The effects tend to be subtle, but they've been observed consistently enough that skin health has become one of the more active areas of carotenoid research.

Oxidative stress and inflammation

Researchers are also studying whether lutein and zeaxanthin's antioxidant effects extend beyond the eyes and skin.

A recent human trial found that supplementation with lutein, zeaxanthin, and meso-zeaxanthin reduced several inflammatory cytokines and biomarkers associated with oxidative cardiovascular processes.25 These findings are encouraging because they suggest lutein and zeaxanthin may influence the biological processes involved in healthy aging.

That said, improvements in laboratory biomarkers don't always translate into real-world health outcomes. For now, the strongest and most consistent evidence for lutein and zeaxanthin remains their role in supporting long-term eye health.

Lutein and zeaxanthin dosage

If you're wondering how much lutein and zeaxanthin you actually need, the research provides a useful benchmark. Most clinical trials showing benefits for eye health have used 10mg of lutein and 2mg of zeaxanthin per day.4 5

Those are also the doses used in the landmark AREDS2 trial, which helped establish them as the clinical standard for supporting long-term eye health. More recent studies on digital eye strain and cognitive function have largely stuck with that same formula, giving us a great deal of confidence in those amounts.6 7

You will, however, find supplements that provide considerably more. Nutricost, for example, contains 20mg of lutein and 4mg of zeaxanthin per serving. Researchers have studied doses in this range and found them to be well tolerated. While some studies have reported changes in biomarkers such as MPOD, those findings haven't consistently translated into better vision or other clinical outcomes than the 10mg/2mg combination used in AREDS2 and many subsequent trials.26

There is currently no Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for lutein or zeaxanthin. Based on the available evidence, 10mg of lutein and 2mg of zeaxanthin remains the best-supported daily dose for most people interested in maintaining long-term eye health. That doesn't mean higher doses are a bad choice — they simply haven't been shown to work better.

Who are lutein and zeaxanthin supplements for?

People concerned about long-term eye health

People with high screen time

If you spend much of your day looking at a computer, tablet, or smartphone, lutein and zeaxanthin may help support visual comfort. Recent clinical trials in heavy screen users have reported improvements in macular pigment, visual comfort, tear-film stability, and certain aspects of visual performance after several months of supplementation.6 22

People interested in supporting skin health

Like the eyes, the skin is constantly exposed to oxidative stress from sunlight. Studies suggest lutein and zeaxanthin may help improve skin hydration, elasticity, and resistance to UV-induced damage after several months of consistent supplementation.9 10 24 46

Older adults interested in healthy cognitive aging

Although the evidence isn't as strong as it is for eye health, several randomized trials suggest lutein and zeaxanthin may support areas such as visual memory, learning, attention, and neural processing speed.7 8 Larger studies are still needed, but the findings are encouraging.

Who are they not for?

Lutein and zeaxanthin are among the best-researched nutrients for supporting long-term eye health, but they're not the right fit for everyone. The following groups may want to reconsider supplementation or adjust their expectations.

People expecting immediate results

Lutein and zeaxanthin don't work overnight. Because they gradually build up in tissues like the retina, most clinical studies report benefits after several months of consistent daily use rather than days or weeks.4 5 If you're looking for an instant improvement in vision or eye comfort, these supplements will likely disappoint.

People expecting sharper eyesight

Lutein and zeaxanthin help support retinal health, but they won't eliminate the need for glasses or contacts. Most clinical studies report improvements in contrast sensitivity, glare recovery, and other measures of visual performance — not sharper visual acuity.20 21

Lutein and zeaxanthin supplement FAQ

When is the best time to take lutein and zeaxanthin?

There's no evidence that one time of day is better than another. What matters most is taking them consistently. Because lutein and zeaxanthin are fat-soluble carotenoids, they're best absorbed when taken with a meal that contains some dietary fat, such as olive oil, avocado, nuts, or eggs.14 28

Should I take lutein and zeaxanthin with food?

Yes. Taking lutein and zeaxanthin with food — particularly a meal containing healthy fats — can improve their absorption. Many supplements also suspend these carotenoids in oil-based softgels for the same reason, helping increase their bioavailability compared to dry formulations.14 28

How long does it take for lutein and zeaxanthin to work?

Unlike some supplements, lutein and zeaxanthin don't produce immediate or even short-term effects. Because they gradually accumulate in the retina, most clinical studies report measurable improvements after about three to six months of consistent daily supplementation. Benefits such as increased MPOD, improved contrast sensitivity, and better visual comfort develop gradually rather than overnight.4 5 6 20

Can lutein and zeaxanthin improve eyesight?

Not in the way many people hope. Research suggests lutein and zeaxanthin can improve measures such as contrast sensitivity, glare recovery, and overall visual performance, but they haven't consistently been shown to improve visual acuity or eliminate the need for glasses or contact lenses.4 5 20 21

Can you get enough lutein and zeaxanthin from food?

It's possible, but many people don't. Leafy greens such as spinach and kale are excellent sources, as are egg yolks, corn, peas, and pistachios. However, typical Western diets often provide considerably less lutein and zeaxanthin than the amounts used in clinical studies, which is one reason supplementation has become increasingly popular.14 17

Can I take lutein and zeaxanthin every day?

Yes. Human studies have safely evaluated daily supplementation for months and even years, and both carotenoids are considered well tolerated at the doses commonly used in supplements. Side effects are uncommon and are generally limited to occasional mild digestive discomfort.3 4 26 27 47 48
