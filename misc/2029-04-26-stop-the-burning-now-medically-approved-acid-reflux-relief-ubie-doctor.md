What Is Acid Reflux?

Acid reflux happens when stomach acid flows backward into the esophagus—the tube that connects your mouth to your stomach.

Normally, a muscle at the bottom of the esophagus (called the lower esophageal sphincter, or LES) acts like a valve. It opens to let food in and then closes tightly. When this muscle weakens or relaxes at the wrong time, stomach acid can move upward, irritating the lining of your esophagus.

That irritation causes the familiar:

Burning in the chest (heartburn)

Sour or bitter taste in the mouth

Regurgitation of food or liquid

Discomfort after meals

Symptoms that worsen when lying down

Occasional reflux after a large meal is common. But frequent acid reflux (more than twice a week) may indicate GERD.

Medically Approved Next Steps

If you're experiencing constant acid reflux, here's what evidence-based medicine recommends.

1. Start With Lifestyle Adjustments

For many people, small changes significantly reduce symptoms.

Try the following:

Eat smaller, more frequent meals

Avoid lying down for at least 2–3 hours after eating

Elevate the head of your bed by 6–8 inches

Maintain a healthy weight

Stop smoking

Limit alcohol

Identify and reduce trigger foods

These steps are often recommended as first-line treatment by gastroenterology guidelines.

2. Consider Over-the-Counter Medications (Short-Term)

If lifestyle changes aren't enough, short-term medications may help:

Antacids – Neutralize existing stomach acid

H2 blockers – Reduce acid production

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) – Stronger acid suppression

PPIs are commonly used for moderate to severe acid reflux and GERD. However, they should not be taken long-term without medical supervision. Long-term use may carry risks, including nutrient deficiencies and increased infection risk.

Always read labels carefully and speak with a pharmacist or doctor if unsure.

3. Know the Warning Signs

While most acid reflux is manageable, certain symptoms require prompt medical evaluation.

Seek medical care right away if you experience:

Chest pain that spreads to the arm, neck, or jaw

Shortness of breath

Vomiting blood

Black or tarry stools

Difficulty swallowing

Unexplained weight loss

Persistent vomiting

Chest burning can sometimes mimic heart-related pain. If there is any doubt, it's safest to seek emergency evaluation.

When to Speak to a Doctor

You should schedule a medical appointment if:

Acid reflux happens more than twice per week

Symptoms wake you at night

Over-the-counter treatments aren't helping

You need medication regularly

You're over 50 and experiencing new symptoms

A doctor may recommend:

Prescription medications

An upper endoscopy

pH monitoring

Testing for related conditions

These evaluations are routine and help guide proper treatment.

If anything feels severe, sudden, or life-threatening, seek immediate medical care. It's always better to rule out serious causes than to wait.

Practical Daily Tips for Managing Acid Reflux

Here are realistic habits that often make a noticeable difference:

Finish dinner earlier in the evening

Walk for 10–15 minutes after meals

Wear loose-fitting clothing

Avoid heavy late-night snacks

Reduce stress (stress can worsen symptoms perception)

Sleep on your left side (this may reduce nighttime reflux)

Small, consistent habits matter more than drastic short-term changes.
