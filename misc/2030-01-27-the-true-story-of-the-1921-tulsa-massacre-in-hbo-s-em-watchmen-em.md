# The True Story of the 1921 Tulsa Massacre in HBO's <em>Watchmen</em>

Spoiler for the opening scene of the Watchmen series debut. If you haven't watched yet, this article will describe key points of the show's first episode.

You’ve probably never heard of the Tulsa Race Massacre of 1921 that was depicted in the opening scenes of HBO’s Watchmen—you may have even chalked the event up as some good old comic fiction. But the reality of the fact is that Watchmen's scenes are actually based on a real story, and one that's been hidden from public perception from basically the moment it happened; after the event occurred, leaving hundreds dead, the Tulsa Tribune buried the story from the front page and police and militia documents went missing.

Considered one of the worst instances of racial violence in American history, it took scholars and educators decades to identify the full extent of the riots; the event was rarely mentioned in history textbooks, and only in 2009 was the riot included in Oklahoma history books.

Watchmen showrunner and series creator Damon Lindelof said that part of the inspiration for his series was Ta-Nehisi Coates' article, The Case for Reparations. That was the first time Lindelof heard about the Tulsa massacre. (And given the show's allusions to reparations made after Tulsa’s violent racial history, it’s clear the series has something to say on the topic of historical justice.)

What happened during the Tulsa Massacre?

Over the course of 18 hours between May 31 and June 1, 1921, a white mob attacked residents of the Greenwood neighborhood of Tulsa, Oklahoma, destroying homes and shops, and killing hundreds of people. Greenwood was then a primarily black community and included a prosperous business district known as “Black Wall Street.” The city itself, though the beneficiary of an influx of oil money, maintained high crime rates and vigilantism (or, rather, mob justice).

During the violence, some 1,200 houses were burned and hundreds more looted. The National Guard eventually arrived, but the riot had run its course.

Why did the mob form?

Tulsa was a highly segregated city with the majority of its 10,000 black citizens residing in Greenwood. When a black teenager, Dick Rowland, was arrested after entering an elevator with a white female operator—who screamed, prompting Rowland to flee—a group of black WWI Army veterans armed themselves and stood outside the courthouse; they feared, rightly, that a lynch mob would attack the courthouse before any trial. The 75 citizens were met by some 1,000 whites, many armed, who demanded the release of Rowland into their own custody. At that point, the shooting began. The looting and burning came not long afterwards.
