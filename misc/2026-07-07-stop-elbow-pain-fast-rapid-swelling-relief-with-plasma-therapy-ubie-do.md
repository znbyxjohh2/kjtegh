Explanation

Elbow bursitis occurs when the small fluid-filled sacs (bursae) around your elbow become inflamed, leading to pain, swelling and limited movement. Conventional treatments—rest, ice, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspiration or corticosteroid injections—often help, but some people continue to experience discomfort or prefer to avoid repeated steroids. Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy is an emerging option designed to harness your body's own healing factors to reduce swelling, ease pain and promote long-term recovery.

Below, we explore how PRP for elbow bursitis works, what to expect, who may benefit—and when you should always check in with a medical professional.

Understanding Elbow Bursitis

What is a bursa?A bursa is a tiny sac filled with lubricating fluid that reduces friction between bone, tendons and skin.

Olecranon bursitis (the most common type) affects the bursa at the tip of the elbow (olecranon).

Symptoms include:

Visible swelling or puffiness at the back of the elbow

Dull, aching pain—often worse when leaning on the elbow

Redness or warmth if infection is present (septic bursitis)

Restricted elbow movement

Causes:

Repetitive pressure or trauma (e.g., leaning on hard surfaces)

Inflammatory conditions (gout, rheumatoid arthritis)

Infection from a cut or insect bite

Overuse in sports or manual labor

How PRP Therapy Works

Blood DrawA small sample (30–60 mL) of your blood is taken from a vein in your arm.

CentrifugationThe sample is spun in a centrifuge to separate red blood cells from plasma enriched with platelets.

Platelet ConcentrateThe plasma layer containing platelets and growth factors is collected.

InjectionUnder ultrasound guidance, your doctor injects the PRP directly into or around the inflamed bursa.

Who Might Benefit Most?

Individuals with chronic (>3 months) elbow bursitis resistant to standard care

People seeking to minimize steroid or NSAID use

Athletes or manual laborers aiming for faster, more robust tissue repair

Patients without active infection or significant bleeding disorders

PRP may be less suitable if you have:

A blood-borne infection (e.g., hepatitis, HIV)

Severe anemia or low platelet count

Active cancer

An infected bursa (septic bursitis requires antibiotics or aspiration first)

Complementary and Alternative Therapies

While PRP can play a central role, consider integrating:

Physical therapy for strengthening and posture correction

Bracing or elbow pads to off-load pressure

Activity modification (e.g., ergonomic workspace adjustments)

Low-impact exercises (swimming, yoga) to maintain flexibility

Nutritional support (anti-inflammatory diet, adequate protein)

Combining approaches often yields the best long-term outcomes.

When to Seek Medical Attention

Elbow bursitis is rarely life-threatening, but certain signs warrant prompt evaluation:

Fever, chills or redness spreading beyond the elbow (possible infection)

Intense, unremitting pain not relieved by prescribed measures

Numbness, tingling or weakness in the arm or hand

Sudden, severe swelling after a direct trauma
