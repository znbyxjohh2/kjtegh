# Chris Heria Shares the Inspiration Behind His Bodyweight Workout Empire

Before he had more than 3.5 million subscribers watching him doing muscle-ups and backflips on his YouTube channel, Chris Heria got inspired to get shredded by watching Dragon Ball-Z.

“Those types of cartoons that had bodyweight training or just pushing your body to its limits. That inspired me,” he says. “There's one episode of Dragon Ball-Z where Goku goes to this planet where there's way more gravity. He can barely walk when he first gets there. He can barely crawl. But then after a while, he's running around chasing this monkey, and then he gets used to that extra weight… or that extra gravity."

Today, Heria looks like he’s gone Super Saiyan himself, as his YouTube fans and subscribers to his HeriaPro workout app can attest. He spoke with us about meals to get shredded, how to keep calisthenics from getting stale, and offered his tips to finally get that first muscleup.

Lots of guys have been doing home bodyweight workouts since the start of lockdowns last year. If those routines start to get a little stale, what are some things they can try out that you do to help spice things up?

The beautiful thing about calisthenics is that every movement has its own progression tree. So technically, things don't really get stale for a while because they go from one progression to the other. This could take a couple of months of training, depending on the progression. It could take a couple of years.

For example, when you're doing regular pushups, you're using a certain percentage of your body weight. But then as you go on to progressions like planche pushups, you're actually pushing your entire body weight. And so it's a pretty drastic change. The angle makes it a lot more difficult as well. So to answer that question, as long as you're working up your progressions, that's the main goal. It's not necessarily to focus on your pullups or pushups. Increasing the volume of those, increasing your repetitions ... that's going to help you move on to the harder progression. The main goal is to always be trying to move on to the next progression because that's where your growth lies.

Another way you progress is wearing weighted vests. How did you get started doing that? And what do you like about that versus other external loading?

I've been using a weight vest since I first started my calisthenics journey back in 2011. One of the main ways I guess I got into the weight vest was seeing like Dragon Ball-Z and those types of cartoons that had body weight training or just pushing your body to its limits. That inspired me.

There's one episode of Dragon Ball-Z where Goku goes to this planet where there's way more gravity. He can barely walk when he first gets there. He can barely crawl. But then after a while, he's running around chasing this monkey, and then he gets used to that extra weight…or that extra gravity. That was one of the main things that inspired me to do that.

I feel like that was definitely a big influence for me. Dragon Ball-Z was definitely one of my favorites. So was Naruto: There's another guy on there called Rock Lee, and he does a lot of calisthenics training as well. So he would do a lot of his training with ankle weights and weights on his body and hands and stuff like that. Then when he takes them off, he's super, super fast.

One thing that can be missing from a lot of at-home, bodyweight workouts is horizontal pulling. What are some lower difficulty exercises that you work in with your calisthenics routines that get you that horizontal pull?

Body rows [a.k.a. inverted rows]. Those are the basic one. If you're doing them with bodyweight, it's a different type of contraction [than doing rows in the gym with weight]. You're using a lot more stabilization muscles. The way you can do them inside your house is by putting two chairs together and bending your knees. You can start with rows like that. And if you want to take it to the next level, you can elevate your feet and put your feet on top of a chair as well. So now you have three chairs, you know, all with their feet elevated as well, and then you can row.

The whole point is to try to row up all the way up to your your delts or your chest, if you can. People that don't often train calisthenics consistently probably don't even have that type of range of motion.

You posted a treadmill sprint workout in August for getting shredded. Are there other sprint routines that you're using right now?

What I've been doing recently is running with the weight vest on. I'm trying to slowly get to the level where I was without the [38-pound or 45-pound] weight vest on—but now with the weight on. Doing sprints, running on inclines, and gradually working my pace up.

One calisthenics move that a lot of guys want to try to get is the muscleup. If they’re already pretty strong on pullups, what’s the secret to getting that first muscleup?

It's really all technique. You know, there's so many people walking around right now that maybe have never done a muscleup in their life, and they 100 percent have the strength to do it... or are even overqualified. They just don't have the technique right.

A lot of people, when they're attempting to muscleup just by looking at the way that it looks, they just try to pull up as high as they can. It's not about pulling straight up. Because that's going to hit the bar to your chest and you're not going to be able to go around and over the bar.

Basically, you want to pull the bar to your body as if it's a row, to get it to your belly button. That's going to get you more range of motion. Once you bring it to your abdomen area, you want to lean over the bar and then begin to push it down. So you're making more of a "C" motion versus an "I" motion like a pullup. So a lot of people make that mistake, and they think that they're going to pull straight up and get into a muscleup. They actually end up hitting their chest or shoulders on the bar.

Instead of pulling from your hands up to your ears, like like maybe most people do with a pullup, you want to do a motion like you're doing a body row, the opposite of a pushup. Once you pull up and get your scapula in that position, then you can begin to round over the bar.

You’ve posted a lot of videos with morning routines. What’s your morning fitness routine right now? What’s the thing you do every day to stay strong and shredded?

I've done all of those [routines in the videos] at one time or another. [My morning routine] can vary depending on what I have going on—it could be boxing training, something else. But one thing I always do every morning, no matter what, is 100 pushups every morning. It's worked phenomenally for me. You can't underestimate the power of consistency. Eventually, those 100 pushups feel like nothing, and you're doing 100 reps in a row.

I actually have my [10-year old] son doing it as well. I started him off doing it with elevated pushups against the countertop in my bathroom. We'd it in sets—20s, or something like that. Each day, I would lower the elevation lower and lower. And now he's already doing 100 pushups on the ground.
